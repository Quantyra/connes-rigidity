                                       Abstract
    We disprove the recent OpenAI, Anthropic, and Zhou “disproofs” by counterex-
ample to Connes’ rigidity conjecture, then show the same move proves the conjecture
itself.
    Part I shows that three independent claimed counterexamples to the conjecture
are invalid. The OpenAI [33] and Anthropic [21] counterexamples construct pairs of
group extensions—one split (Γ0 ), one non-split (Γτ )—of Sp4 (Z) by Z4 and claim the
corresponding group von Neumann algebras are isomorphic. The concurrent Zhou
construction [31] takes a different approach: two semidirect products Γi = D ⋊θi H
sharing the same fiber and quotient but with distinct actions, and claims L(Γ1 ) ∼
                                                                                 = L(Γ2 )
via Pontryagin duality. All three are invalid.
    The primary argument is a reductio ad absurdum that is purely mathematical: it
does not rely on any semantic mismatch between code labels and mathematical objects,
on whether the ICC or property-(T) certificates in the Lean code are valid or invalid,
or on any code-level observation whatsoever. The argument assumes L(Γ0 ) ∼      = L(Γτ ),
equips both groups with Bernoulli actions (chosen by us, not inherited from either
counterexample), and applies the twisted comultiplication and Ioana’s classification
(Theorem 8.2, JAMS 2011) to produce an injective group homomorphism δ2 : Γ0 ,→ Γτ .
The Borel density theorem forces δ2 to preserve the fiber Z4 ; Margulis superrigidity
forces an inner automorphism on the quotient Sp4 (Z); Schur’s lemma forces a scalar
map on the fiber; and the resulting cohomological identity α∗ [τ ] = [τ ] = 0 contra-
dicts the non-vanishing of the Bockstein class (Proposition 3.5 of [21]). By reductio,

the published theorems of Ioana, Borel, Margulis, and Schur.
     The reductio is robust to alternate readings of the Lean type signatures—e.g. tak-
ing the quotient to be a congruence kernel K = ker(SL4 (Z[t]) → SL4 (F3 )), the fiber
modules to be F2 [t]-types, the CarryGroup law to produce exponent-4 torsion, or the
shiftedCarry cocycle to be F2 -valued. If the finished groups satisfy the structural
hypotheses (higher-rank lattice, irreducible action, nonzero extension class), the chain
applies verbatim; if they do not, the groups fail ICC or property (T) and cannot con-
stitute a counterexample in the first place.
     Independently of the reductio, the OpenAI formalisation exhibits three code-internal
failures, each independently fatal: (i) a specification gap (the code constructs cocycle
extensions D×c K but verifies ICC using orbit arguments valid only for semidirect prod-
ucts D ⋊ K); (ii) the zero-cocycle group has a nontrivial centre, destroying ICC; and
(iii) the same group surjects onto an infinite abelian group, destroying property (T).
These are direct observations about the defined Lean objects; they require no classical
theorem and no identification with the Anthropic groups.
     The Zhou construction [31] falls to the same reductio methodology. The subgroup
D is the unique maximal abelian normal subgroup of both Γ1 and Γ2 ; any injective
homomorphism δ2 : Γ1 ,→ Γ2 must preserve it. The restriction to D must then inter-
twine the two H-module structures θ1 and θ2 . But Zhou’s own non-isomorphism proof
(Section 6 of [31]) shows that no such intertwining exists: θ1 is semisimple while θ2 is
not. The reductio uses Zhou’s own group-theoretic argument to contradict his algebra
isomorphism claim.

Part I
Disproof of the Counterexamples
1    Why Connes’ Rigidity Matters
In algebraic quantum field theory, a gauge theory is specified by its net of observable alge-
bras — von Neumann algebras assigned to spacetime regions satisfying the Haag–Kastler
axioms [19]. The structure group G of the theory is not an independent input; it is recovered
from the algebra. The Doplicher–Haag–Roberts reconstruction theorem [20] demonstrates
that the compact gauge group can be extracted from the superselection structure of the ob-
servables. This reconstruction presupposes that the map from groups to observable algebras
is faithful: distinct groups must produce distinguishable algebras.
    Connes’ rigidity conjecture asserts that this presupposition holds for ICC property-(T)
groups. If it fails, the inverse problem — given an observable algebra, determine the structure
group — is ill-posed. If L(G) ∼  = L(H) with G ≁= H, the group von Neumann algebra does not
determine the group. The functor from groups to algebras fails to be faithful, and no inverse
functor exists. The structure group becomes an unrecoverable datum: no measurement, no
observable, no experiment can distinguish G from H, because the algebras of observables
are identical.
    The consequences propagate through the geometric framework. The structure group
determines the space of connections on a principal bundle: a connection is a g-valued 1-form


                                                3
satisfying a G-equivariance condition. If the structure group is ambiguous, the connection
space is ambiguous. Gauge equivalence — the identification of connections related by G-
valued gauge transformations — depends on G. The moduli space of gauge-inequivalent
connections, which is the configuration space of the quantum theory, is the quotient of
the connection space by the gauge group. If the gauge group is not determined by the
observables, the quotient is not determined by the observables. The path integral measure,
which integrates over this quotient, inherits the ambiguity.
    In the language of principal bundles: a principal G-bundle P → M has a structure group
G acting freely and transitively on each fiber. The bundle determines its base, its total
space, and its projection. The question is whether it also determines its structure group. If
two non-isomorphic groups G and H can serve as structure groups for bundles producing
identical observable algebras, the fiber is not a physical observable — it is an artifact of
notation. The choice of G is a convention, and the principal bundle does not carry enough
information to recover it.
    Connes’ rigidity, if true, eliminates this pathology. For rigid groups, L(G) ∼
                                                                                 = L(H) =⇒
   ∼
G = H: the observable algebra determines the structure group uniquely. The principal
bundle determines its own fiber. The connection space, the gauge equivalence relation, and
the moduli space are all determined by the observables. The inverse problem is well-posed.
The self-consistency of the algebraic foundations of gauge theory therefore depends on the
conjecture. The stakes of the counterexamples analysed below, and of the positive proof in
Part II, are physical as well as mathematical.
    There is, moreover, an empirical argument. The deduction of gauge groups from mea-
surement works. The 1/r2 law of the electromagnetic force determines, via the structure
of the Maxwell equations and the associated fiber bundle, that the gauge group is U (1) —
the circle. This is not a convention; it is an observable consequence of the fall-off rate, the
superselection structure, and the representation theory of the observable algebra. The same
inverse deduction recovers SU (2) × U (1) from the electroweak sector and SU (3) from the
strong force. If Connes’ rigidity were false, these deductions would be ambiguous: non-
isomorphic groups could produce identical observable algebras, and the gauge group would
be underdetermined by any experiment. The fact that gauge groups are empirically recov-
erable — that the inverse problem has a unique solution in every case physics has tested —
constitutes physical evidence that the functor from groups to algebras is faithful. The AI
counterexamples claim to overturn this faithfulness. Physics says otherwise.

Remark 1 (Scope and precision of the physical argument). Several technical distinctions
sharpen the physical picture. (i) The local observable algebras in algebraic QFT are type III1
factors (Buchholz–Fredenhagen), not the type II1 factors L(G) that appear in Connes’ con-
jecture. The counterexamples operate at the II1 level — this is the choice made by OpenAI
and Anthropic, not by us. The physical point is that if the functor G 7→ L(G) fails to be
faithful at the simplest level (II1 factors from ICC groups), there is no reason to expect
faithfulness at any higher level; the information-theoretic leak at the II1 foundation propa-
gates upward through crossed products and second quantisation. (ii) The Doplicher–Roberts
reconstruction (1989) recovers a compact group from the symmetric tensor C ∗ -category of
superselection sectors. Whether this is called the “gauge group” or the “global symmetry
group of the first kind” is a terminological choice; the mathematical content is that a group

2    Overview
The two claimed counterexamples [33, 21] are the same construction: both build a split
extension Γ0 and a non-split extension Γτ of Sp4 (Z) by Z4 and claim L(Γ0 ) ∼   = L(Γτ ). The
OpenAI formalisation is a Lean encoding of the Anthropic groups. The primary argument of
Part I is a reductio ad absurdum (Section 9, Theorem 5) that applies to both simultaneously:
the assumed algebra isomorphism, combined with Ioana’s classification (JAMS 2011) and
a four-step cohomological argument (Borel density, Margulis superrigidity, Schur’s lemma,
non-vanishing of the Bockstein class), produces a contradiction. The algebra isomorphism
cannot exist. The underlying mathematical claim of both counterexamples is false.
    The OpenAI formalisation additionally exhibits a specification gap at the code level,
which we analyse in Sections 2–8. The code constructs cocycle extensions but verifies ICC
using orbit arguments valid only for semidirect products. This analysis provides independent,
code-level evidence for the failure of the counterexample, but the reductio of Section 9 is the
primary and unconditional argument.


3    Statement of the Conjecture
To any countable group G one can associate its group von Neumann algebra L(G), defined
as the weak operator closure of the complex group algebra C[G] acting by left convolution
on ℓ2 (G) [1]. A classical result of Murray and von Neumann [1] establishes that L(G) is a
II1 factor if and only if G is ICC—that is, every non-identity conjugacy class of G is infinite.
    In [4], Connes discovered that II1 factors associated with ICC groups possessing Kazh-
dan’s property (T) [3] exhibit strong rigidity under small perturbations; in particular, both
the fundamental group and the outer automorphism group of every such factor are countable.
These results motivated Connes to pose the following conjecture [5]:

      Connes’ Rigidity Conjecture. Let Γ be a countable discrete ICC group with
      Kazhdan’s property (T). If Λ is any countable discrete group such that

                                        L(Γ) ∼
                                             = L(Λ)

      as von Neumann algebras, then Γ ∼
                                      = Λ.

Since property (T) for groups is a von Neumann algebra invariant by Connes–Jones [6], the
conjecture can equivalently be stated as: every ICC property-(T) group is W∗ -superrigid.

                                               5
Remarkable progress toward this conjecture has been made through Popa’s deformation/-
rigidity theory [7], culminating in the first examples of W∗ -superrigid groups [8].
    The ICC hypothesis is not a technicality; it is what ensures that L(Γ) is a factor. In
particular, the centre Z(Γ) must be trivial: any non-identity central element z has conjugacy
class {z}, which is finite.

5     Proof that the Centres are Non-Trivial
We treat the two groups separately. The first case is immediate; the second requires a short
argument.

Case 1: the zero cocycle (Γ0 )
The CarryGroup construction bundles the action and the carry together: the shiftedCarry
term contributes to both the 2-cocycle and the action of K on the module D = V ⊕ B. The
action on V is the standard linear action of Sp(2n, Z) (independent of the carry), but the
action on the B-component is carry-dependent. When the carry is set to zero:

     The carry-dependent part of the action on B vanishes. Elements of the form (0, b, 1)
      (zero V -component, nonzero B-component, identity in K) become central: conjugation
      by (0, 0, k) acts trivially on the B-component when the carry terms are absent.


                                               6
     Any non-identity central element has a conjugacy class of size 1, which violates the
      ICC condition.
More precisely, the orbit lemmas in the code (k D orbit infinite, kEAddAction orbit infinite of exac
verify infinite orbits via exact-sequence decompositions that track the K-action on V and
B separately through Pontryagin duality. The orbit on V uses the standard linear action
(nontrivial regardless of carry). The orbit on B uses the carry-dependent action. When carry
= 0, the B-orbits collapse to singletons, producing central elements. The ICC certificate lifts
the orbit results from both components, but the B-component orbits are valid only for the
nonzero-carry group.
    The zero-cocycle group therefore contains a nontrivial central subgroup (the pure B-
elements) and is not ICC. A single non-identity central element suffices; this group has
infinitely many.

Case 2: the twisted cocycle (Γ1 )
For the twisted cocycle c1 ̸= 0, the situation is more delicate. In a general cocycle extension
Ec , conjugation acts by the linear Γ-action:
                               (b, g) · ι(a) · (b, g)−1 = ι(g · a),
so a lattice element ι(a) is central if and only if a is fixed by every element of Sp(2n, Z).
Under the standard symplectic representation, which is irreducible over Q, the fixed-point
submodule ΛΓ is {0}, and this line of argument does not yield central elements.
   Whether the twisted extension Ec1 is itself ICC therefore depends on finer details of the
cocycle and the module structure—details that the Lean formalisation may resolve but that
we have not independently verified from the code.
   This does not affect the conclusion. A counterexample to Connes’ rigidity conjecture
requires a pair of ICC property-(T) groups with isomorphic group von Neumann algebras.
Case 1 already shows that Γ0 is not ICC. A pair in which even one group fails to be ICC lies
outside the hypothesis of the conjecture, regardless of the status of the other.

Summary
The zero-cocycle group Γ0 has infinite centre and is therefore not ICC. This alone suffices: the
pair (Γ0 , Γ1 ) cannot constitute a counterexample to a conjecture whose hypothesis requires
both groups to be ICC.

7    The von Neumann Algebra Isomorphism
The formalisation further exhibits a trace-preserving ∗-isomorphism
                                     L(Γ0 ) ≃ L(Γ1 ).
Such an isomorphism is compatible with—indeed, expected in the presence of—a common
infinite centre (the centre of the group von Neumann algebra contains the group algebra of

                                             9
the centre of the group [1]). However, because the groups themselves fail to be ICC, the pair
(Γ0 , Γ1 ) lies outside the class of groups to which Connes’ conjecture applies.
    This observation also explains why recent positive results on rigidity for groups with
infinite centre—notably those of Chifan, Fernández Quero, Osin, and Tan [9]—are formulated
as extensions of the original conjecture rather than as resolutions of it: the original statement
simply does not cover those groups.


8      A Further Obstruction: Failure of Property (T)
As a further observation, the zero-cocycle group faces a second, independent obstruction.
The central subgroup identified above (the pure B-elements) generates an abelian quotient:
the projection onto the B-factor, which is a group homomorphism when the carry-dependent
action vanishes, maps the zero-cocycle group onto an infinite abelian group. Property-(T)
groups have finite abelianization [3, 15], so the zero-cocycle group cannot have prop-
erty (T).
     This means the zero-cocycle group fails both hypotheses of Connes’ rigidity conjecture:
it is not ICC (because its centre is non-trivial) and it does not have property (T) (because
it surjects onto Z2n ). The claimed counterexample does not get off the ground under the
standard statement of the conjecture.


9      Conclusion
The specification gap: three distinct constructions
The error in the formalisation can be stated precisely as a conflation of three group-theoretic
constructions that are distinct objects with different properties:
    1. The direct product D × K (trivial action, trivial cocycle). This is a trivial bundle: the
       factors commute; D ≤ Z(D × K). The group is not ICC if D ̸= {1}, and it does not
       have property (T) if D is infinite abelian.
    2. The semidirect product D ⋊φ K (nontrivial action φ, trivial cocycle). This is a non-
       trivial bundle with a flat connection. The multiplication is (d1 , k1 )(d2 , k2 ) = (d1 +
       φ(k1 )(d2 ), k1 k2 ). If K acts on D with infinite orbits on every nonzero element, and K
       is ICC, then D⋊φ K can be ICC. The transvection orbit arguments in the formalisation
       are valid for this construction.
    3. The cocycle extension D ×c K (action φ, nontrivial 2-cocycle c : K × K → D). This
       is a twisted bundle with a curved connection. The multiplication is (d1 , k1 )(d2 , k2 ) =

curved bundle has it. The ICC property depends on the full group multiplication, including
the cocycle, not merely on the action.
    This is the specification problem in its sharpest form. The Lean code correctly verifies
that certain orbits are infinite under the action φ. But the group whose ICC property matters
is not D ⋊φ K — it is D ×c K with the shiftedCarry cocycle. The certificate proves the
right property for the wrong group.

Summary
The construction yields two non-isomorphic property-(T) groups with isomorphic group von
Neumann algebras. At least one of them (the zero-cocycle group) possesses a non-trivial
centre and is therefore not ICC. They consequently furnish no counterexample to Connes’
rigidity conjecture as it is standardly stated [5].
    The claimed disproof is invalid.
    This case illustrates a failure mode that is becoming increasingly well documented in the
literature on AI-assisted formal mathematics: the gap between what a formal proof verifies
and what it means. The Lean kernel certifies that a proof term inhabits a given type; it
does not certify that the type faithfully encodes the intended mathematical claim. As Tao
has emphasised, “verification certifies the formal statement, not that it matches intent—so
human review is reduced, not eliminated,” and the emerging best practice “divides trust
so that humans author (or carefully review) the statements of theorems while automation
handles the proofs” [10].
    A recent audit of five widely used Lean theorem-proving benchmarks surfaced 4,833
findings—including counterexamples, vacuous theorems, and unsound axioms—all of which
had passed machine verification [11]. In AI-assisted formalisation of statistical learning the-
ory, the “most dangerous failure mode” encountered was “not a failed proof but a successful
proof of a false statement”—cases where “only a human, by constructing explicit counterex-
amples, detected that the statement was wrong” [12]. Similarly, work on autoformalization
of tensor networks found that “the system can produce formally correct proofs of statements
that are weaker or more special than the intended theorem,” necessitating a “human review
loop . . . designed to catch this drift by checking . . . for mathematical meaning, not only for
formal consistency” [13].
    The claimed disproof of Connes’ rigidity conjecture is a case in point. The formalisa-
tion may correctly establish every algebraic and analytic claim it makes. What it does not
establish—and what the Lean kernel cannot check—is that those claims bear on the conjec-
ture as stated. A human reading the conjecture notices the ICC hypothesis; a proof assistant,

                                              11
given groups that are not ICC, will happily verify whatever is asserted about them.
    AI-driven proofs are not immune to error. They must be checked by humans—not at
the level of individual proof steps, where the kernel is reliable, but at the level of problem
specification, where it is silent. Human-steered formalisation, in which a mathematician
formulates and audits the theorem statements while AI fills in the proofs, remains superior
to fully autonomous pipelines precisely because it guards against the class of error exhibited
here: a correct proof of the wrong theorem.
    The deeper issue is dispositional. AI systems are, in practice, more like us than we
pretend—and not in the good way. They take shortcuts, make assumptions, and fool them-
selves (and especially their users) into confidence about claims they have not actually verified.
Autonomous agents are particularly prone to unjustified intuitive leaps: they optimise for
task completion, not for truth, and like all natural optimisers they tend to expend as little
energy as possible [14]. A human mathematician confronted with a conjecture reads its
hypotheses because the cost of missing one is professional embarrassment. An autonomous
proof agent, lacking that incentive structure, will happily construct an elaborate formalisa-
tion around groups that do not satisfy the hypotheses it never bothered to check.

Proof of the Theorem
The same mathematical distinction that invalidates the counterexample powers the proof.
The symbol ⋊ denotes a nontrivial semidirect product — a principal bundle with a nontrivial
connection. In Part I, OpenAI’s error was treating a cocycle extension D ×c K (curved
connection) as a semidirect product D ⋊ K (flat connection): the curvature changed the
conjugacy classes and destroyed the ICC property. In Part II, the nontriviality of ⋊ is what
makes the proof work: the Bernoulli crossed product AG = L∞ (X)⋊G is a nontrivial bundle,
which is why the Cartan L∞ (X) does not embed in the group algebra L(G), which is what
verifies the non-intertwining hypothesis (NI2) and forces the full classification to produce the
group isomorphism. If AG were the trivial product L∞ (X) × L(G), the Cartan would embed,
(NI2) would fail, and the proof would not yield the Case (III) decomposition. Nontriviality
of ⋊ is not a technicality; it is the content of the theorem.

Remark 13 (Controlled versus uncontrolled twisting). The twisted comultiplication Θ(ug ) =
ug ⊗ Φ(ug ) deliberately introduces a twist: the second tensor factor Φ(ug ) is not a group uni-
tary in H but an arbitrary element of L(H). This twist is the connection form that encodes
the isomorphism Φ. The crucial difference from the cocycle extension in Part I is one of
control. In the counterexample, the shiftedCarry cocycle alters the group multiplication,
and the formalisation verifies properties (ICC, property (T)) of the untwisted group D ⋊ K
rather than the twisted group D ×c K; the cocycle’s effects on conjugacy classes are unac-
counted for. In the proof, the twist Φ(ug ) is fed directly into Ioana’s classification theorem,
which is designed to handle arbitrary ∗-homomorphisms and to determine exactly what the
twist does: it classifies Θ and extracts group morphisms δ1 , δ2 as output. The twist is not
ignored; it is resolved. Ioana’s theorem is the machine that converts uncontrolled twisting
into controlled group-theoretic data.

In classical topology, the rigidity theorem for principal bundles states: if G is a Lie group
with semisimple Lie algebra g, then H 2 (g, g) = 0 (Whitehead [16]), and every g-bundle is
locally trivial and admits no nontrivial deformations (Kiranagi–Prema–Chidambara [17]).
The structure group is recoverable from the bundle.

components are:

Why torsors do not escape rigidity
Corollary 29 (There is no such thing as a torsor). A torsor is defined as a “group without
a distinguished identity element.” No such object exists. The proof is immediate from the
axioms of group theory.

Proof. A group is a set G with a binary operation satisfying three axioms: associativity,
existence of an identity, and existence of inverses. The identity is not a choice; it is a
consequence. Given any associative binary operation with a left identity and left inverses,
the identity is unique (standard exercise). It is determined by the operation, not selected
from a menu.
    A “torsor for G” is defined as a set X with a free transitive G-action. The claim is that
X “has no preferred basepoint” and therefore “does not determine G.” But X inherits a
ternary operation (x, y, z) 7→ xy −1 z (the unique w such that the action sending y to x sends
z to w). This ternary operation satisfies the heap axioms and determines a group structure
on X: pick any element e ∈ X, define x ·e y := xe−1 y, and ( X, ·e ) is a group isomorphic to
G with identity e.
    The “choice of basepoint” is not a choice of group structure — it is a choice of label for
the identity that already exists. Every element of X is the identity of some group structure
on X, and all these group structures are isomorphic. The isomorphism class is an invariant
of X, not a choice. A torsor does not lack an identity; it has |X| of them, all yielding the
same group up to isomorphism.
    Therefore a torsor is not a new mathematical object. It is a group with the notation
changed. There is no such thing as a group without an identity: the axioms forbid it.
A “group without a preferred identity” is a group whose identity has been temporarily
unnamed, not a group whose identity is absent. The unnamed identity is still determined

                                             33
by the operation. Calling it a “torsor” does not create a new category of objects; it creates
a synonym for “group, viewed through a forgetful functor that discards the name of the
identity while retaining the identity itself.”
   The relevance to Connes’ conjecture is as a corollary, not as an independent argument.
The objection “L(G) is a torsor for G, so G cannot be recovered” rests on the premise
that L(G) might contain non-isomorphic groups H ⊂ U(L(G)) generating the same alge-
bra. Theorem 14 proves this cannot happen for ICC property-(T) groups: every generating
subgroup with property (T) is isomorphic to G. The torsor corollary therefore follows from
the theorem; it is not used in the proof. The algebra L(G) carries the full multiplication
table of G in its algebraic structure, and property (T) makes that table rigid. The twisted
comultiplication reads it out. Ioana’s classification identifies the group.

The proof of Connes’ rigidity conjecture presented here proceeds in four steps: define the
twisted comultiplication Θ (Section 4), verify the non-intertwining hypotheses of Ioana’s

extensions of higher-rank lattices, cohomological rigidity via Margulis superrigidity provides

argument. The twisted comultiplication and Ioana’s classification produce δ2 : G ,→ H from
any assumed algebra isomorphism L(G) ∼     = L(H). The non-intertwining conditions that

Φ is needed. The argument is universal. When the groups are non-isomorphic (Part I), the

When we do not assume non-isomorphism (Part II), the same surjectivity arguments prove
G∼ = H directly. The disproof of every counterexample and the proof of the conjecture are

    The universal form of this argument is stated as Theorem 12 in Part I: for any two non-

normal subgroup and intertwine the two actions, but Zhou’s own Section 6 proves no such
intertwining exists. The argument is robust to alternate readings of the Lean type sig-
natures: any quotient group satisfying the structural hypotheses (higher-rank lattice, irre-
ducible action, nonzero extension class) falls to the same chain, and any quotient failing
them cannot produce a valid counterexample (Remark 8). The physical stakes are resolved:
the Doplicher–Haag–Roberts reconstruction presupposes a faithful functor from groups to
observable algebras, and Connes’ rigidity provides it. The gauge group is a fact about na-
ture, recoverable from the algebra of measurements. The principal bundle determines its own
structure group. The algebraic foundations of gauge theory are self-consistent. The proof
uses no Cartan subalgebra of L(G), bypasses the unique Cartan decomposition problem,
and rests entirely on existing machinery — Ioana’s classification, Margulis superrigidity,
factoriality of II1 factors, and the spectral gap of Bernoulli actions — assembled around
a single new object: the twisted comultiplication. The Bockstein class [τ ] = β[w] serves
as the cohomological invariant that Margulis superrigidity renders indestructible: any in-
jective homomorphism between extensions of higher-rank lattices must preserve it, and its
non-vanishing forces every such homomorphism to be an isomorphism.
    But the proof does more than resolve a conjecture. It demonstrates a method.
    Algebraic topology, the dominant mathematical framework of the twentieth century, uses
algebraic invariants — homology, cohomology, homotopy groups — to study topological
spaces. The direction of information flow is: from topology to algebra. Given a space, one
computes its algebraic invariants.
    The present work inverts this direction. It uses topological structure — bundle rigidity,
connections, holonomy — to determine algebraic objects: groups, von Neumann algebras,
group isomorphisms. The direction of information flow is: from algebra to topology to algebra.
Given an algebraic object (L(G)), one constructs a topological object (the Bernoulli crossed

product, viewed as a measurable principal bundle), reads off its topological invariants (the
holonomy of the twisted comultiplication), and recovers the original algebra (the group G).
    This is topological algebra: the determination of algebraic structure by topological means.
    The forcing principle of Section 3 is the foundational theorem of this field: a bundle forces
a symmetry; a symmetry forces a group; rigidity makes the forcing irreversible. Every step
of the proof of Theorem 14 has both an algebraic description and a topological description:

   Neither column is prior. They are two languages for the same mathematics. The proof

   A necessary consequence: the Cayley–Dickson hierarchy inverts. The bundle is not built
from the group; the group is read off from the bundle. The structure group is not an input to
the construction of a principal bundle; it is an output of the bundle’s rigidity. This inversion
— topology determines algebra, not the reverse — is the content of topological algebra, and
the proof of Connes’ rigidity conjecture is its first theorem.
   The physical consequence is immediate. In algebraic quantum field theory, the Doplicher–
Haag–Roberts reconstruction of the gauge group from the observable algebra presupposes
that the functor from groups to algebras is faithful. Connes’ rigidity establishes this faithful-
ness for ICC property-(T) groups. The principal bundle determines its structure group. The
connection space, gauge equivalence, and moduli space are determined by the observables.
The inverse problem from algebras to groups is well-posed. The algebraic foundations of
gauge theory are self-consistent.
